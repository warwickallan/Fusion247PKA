This is a long-form interview between Greg Isenberg (host) and Nicholas Cole — a ghostwriter/writer the host introduces as having made over $30M writing on the internet, and co-founder of the SaaS platform TypeShare. It is framed by the host as "less like an interview and more a free masterclass" on why most online content is ignored in the AI era, why "AI slop" is misdiagnosed, and how to build what Cole calls a "digital brain" (a personal library of stories, opinions, lessons and ideas) that AI can draw on to keep producing content that is actually yours rather than generic. The episode is built around one continuous argument delivered in stages: SaaS/content only has a durable moat when it carries a point of view; a point of view is built from a personal library of commodity, personality and original content; AI's proper job is to *repeat* that library, not *write* new material from nothing; and voice, timing (timely vs. timeless), and the courage to actually publish are the practical mechanics that make the whole system work. It matters to a reader building products, content or a brand in the AI era because it reframes "AI slop" away from "AI wrote it" and toward "nobody's specific point of view is behind it" — a distinction with direct implications for anyone using AI to write, market, or build a SaaS product.

## What the source says

### 1. The SaaS-moat reversal: "SaaS has no moat" → "SaaS has a moat only with a point of view"
Cole opens by naming a common claim directly — "in the AI era, SaaS no longer has a moat" — and explicitly disagrees: **"I think that SaaS has a moat when you have a point of view."** [03:01] He draws the line between *commodity SaaS* (a product that just accomplishes tasks — "a gazillion scheduling apps," "a gazillion writing apps") and *SaaS with a point of view* (a product built around a specific methodology the user is meant to execute). [03:35] His own example: TypeShare's writing/scheduling features look commodity on the surface, but the product's real goal is to help users "build your personal language model" around a specific point of view about content types (commodity, personality, original — see thread 2). [04:05] **Counterintuitive reversal, stated explicitly by Cole:** a competitor who copies TypeShare's *features* without the underlying point of view will always be "one step, two steps, three steps... behind," because they have to keep looking back at the person who actually did the thinking every time they build a new feature — copying the surface without the philosophy behind it can't compound. [05:00–05:18] His summary line: **"there's no moat in anything if you don't have a point of view. But there's a ton of moat in SaaS and product and writing and marketing if you do have a point of view."** [05:18–05:33]

### 2. The commodity / personality / original content framework, and the AI-slop reframe
Cole builds a foundational premise: language itself is "open source" — nobody owns individual words, or even most groupings of words (his examples: nobody owns "tomato," "catastrophe," or even common first names like "Cole" or "Greg"). [05:33–06:32] **The central reversal of the whole video, stated explicitly:** "AI slop" is commonly blamed on text having been generated by AI. Cole reframes this: **"when you identify, quote-unquote, AI slop, what you're really identifying is commodity content"** — i.e., writing that isn't attributable to any specific individual's point of view, whether a human or an AI wrote it. [06:32–07:12] His illustrating example: "the key to losing weight is eating healthy" is a commodity sentence regardless of who wrote it — nobody accuses the millions of people who've said that sentence of plagiarizing each other; it's simply commodity. [07:12–07:31]

From there he defines three tiers of content, in increasing order of value/differentiation:
- **Commodity content** — writing addressed as "you should..." (imperative, generic advice); low differentiation because it repeats what everybody in a topic already knows. [07:31–08:25]
- **Personality content** — writing addressed from "I" ("I did this," "I think this"); the reader is explicitly consuming *that specific person's* view of the world, not the topic's generic consensus. [08:25–08:45]
- **Original content** — content that crystallizes genuinely new thinking, arrived at only after a long period of writing/thinking in a topic (elaborated in thread 2 continued below).

He explicitly separates *virality* from *value*: content can go viral (5,000–10,000 likes) while being a recycled Wikipedia summary or an AI-generated essay on an already-said take — volume of engagement doesn't establish that content is non-commodity. [09:19–09:48] When Cole creates content he says he optimizes for two things: value to the reader (constantly thinking "how do I help the person in the other seat," which structurally avoids "you should" framing) and affinity (goodwill/trust generated by having actually helped someone). [09:48–11:07]

### 3. The digital brain / personal language model, and "AI should repeat, not write"
Cole traces this idea to his own long-running frustration: 15+ years writing nonfiction online, over a decade writing specifically about how to write online, an estimated 50M+ words written — and a recurring feeling that repeating the same beginner-level advice for the 999th time is "wasting my finite time on earth," even though AI can technically already write that advice as well as he can. [12:03–13:21] This produces his key distinction: **commodity content is not worthless** — it's often the single most helpful thing you can give a beginner — but repeatedly regenerating it yourself is low-leverage, because you're not thinking anything new. [13:45–14:34]

His resolution, stated as the video's core mechanic: **"I don't want the robots writing for me. What I want is the robots repeating me, which is different."** [20:04–20:29] The "digital brain" (used interchangeably with "personal language model") is the growing library of your own already-approved words, ideas, stories and points of view; AI's job is not to generate net-new thinking on your behalf, but to **repeat and recombine what you've already said and already approved**, freeing your finite time for genuinely new (personality/original) thinking instead of rewriting known material. [16:30–17:44] He frames this as the "utopian hope of AI": it automates the low-leverage repetition, which opens time for higher-leverage original thought — with an explicit aside about the societal upside of more people thinking new thoughts instead of "churning out blog posts of things they've already thought about." [16:53–17:44] A concrete use case: rather than manually reposting your best-performing content across platforms (a job people used to hire a social media manager or a VA for), AI can take two of your own existing posts (e.g., two lists of journaling benefits) and recombine them into a new post using only material you've already said and approved. [20:29–21:51]

He also names, unprompted, the two practical bottlenecks to actually doing this (raised by the host, Isenberg, and engaged with directly): (1) capturing ideas properly into a "second brain" tool (Obsidian is named as an example) so they're in a safe, retrievable place, and (2) getting an LLM to draw on that stored material without the output sounding generic/"sloppy." [17:44–18:48] Cole's answer to (2) is effectively the whole rest of the video: give AI your *actual* approved material and your *actual* declared points of view (thread 2), and use voice mechanics (thread 4) deliberately — rather than expecting an LLM to intuit what's "you" from nothing.

### 4. Ghostwriting-derived mechanics: "approved language" and voice as bundled variables
Cole grounds the whole framework in a decade of ghostwriting experience, including for "north of 300 different founders, executives, investors." [57:03] He explains the actual mechanic of ghostwriting, and argues it's the literal template for what AI writing tools should do: a ghostwriter isn't inventing new ideas for a client — they're taking **"approved language"** (things the client has actually said, in a book, a podcast, a meeting recording) and recombining sentences and phrases from different sources into something new. Because the client already said all of those words, the ghostwriter isn't putting new thoughts in their mouth — they're remixing pre-approved material. [15:04–16:30] Cole explicitly states he did "the manual version of what AI can and should do here" — the bigger your library of approved language, the more net-new combinations become possible, without any of it constituting genuinely new, unapproved thinking. [15:54–16:30]

On **voice**: Cole calls it "a bundled term" — something people treat as an intangible, unreplicable quality, when it's actually made of a small number of concrete, swappable variables: word choice (his example: "cool" vs. "exquisite" vs. "fucking dope" each project a different persona for the same idea) and sentence length/structure (a single run-on paragraph vs. short, simple sentences reads as a different "voice"). [53:26–55:08] **Voice is deliberately constructed, not something that "just happens."** [55:08] He illustrates with a ghostwriting anecdote: a client rejected a draft as "doesn't sound like me"; rather than rewriting the piece, Cole diagnosed that the client's real voice leaned on citing studies/facts (not opinion), added two or three "according to [source]" references — changing roughly 1% of the text — and the client was thrilled. [55:08–56:17] He generalizes this into a broader claim about how small the "voice" component of most writing actually is: across his 300+ ghostwriting clients, he mostly reused the same base "authoritative, concise, thought-leadership" voice with small per-person vocabulary swaps, and almost everyone said "this sounds like me." His estimate: a typical piece is roughly 80% commodity groupings of words, with only a minority swapped for subjective/personal adjective and structure choices. [57:03–58:30]

### 5. The timely-vs-timeless value spectrum
Cole introduces a fourth variable — separate from commodity/personality/original — that determines a piece's value profile: **timely vs. timeless.** Timely content has high short-term value and near-zero long-term value; timeless content has low short-term value and high long-term, compounding value. [49:50–50:13] Worked example, built by progressively modifying one script: "here's how to start a newsletter on Substack" is pure commodity; adding "I run a $400,000/year newsletter, here's how" adds a personality/credibility layer; adding "Substack just launched this feature... here's how to start a Substack in 2026" adds timeliness — but that same year-stamped, "just happened" framing is exactly what makes the piece unusable/unrepostable in future years. [48:58–51:35] A second example: an AI-generated meme referencing a specific Apple product announcement captures attention for roughly 24 hours and then has zero further value, versus an essay that took two weeks to write and gets referenced/reused for the next decade. [50:13–51:12] Cole's practical instruction: before creating something, explicitly ask where on this spectrum it sits, and make sure your distribution expectations match — a timeless piece won't get a meme's short-term spike, and a timely piece won't compound. [51:12–51:35]

### 6. Branded content, "the company brain is the moat," and hiring a journalist as content input
Branded (company-account) content, Cole argues, structurally underperforms personal/human content and always has — a lesson he says he was already delivering as a 23-year-old ad-agency employee. [39:35–40:25] But he doesn't conclude branded accounts are useless: there's no reason a brand's account can't repeat *commodity* ideas (which brands do anyway) while leaving *personality* content to actual humans (founders/executives) — giving "double leverage" when a founder builds their own personal language model that the brand account then partially reuses. [40:25–41:39] He extends this with a worked example: rather than Taco Bell's account posting generic "we dropped a new burrito" commodity content, the same message lands with more personality if it's told through a named employee (his example: "Tiffany" at a specific Tulsa store, whose parents met at a Taco Bell) — his broader prediction is that the future of branded content isn't one faceless voice but several named humans behind a brand, each contributing personality content. [41:39–43:31]

The host (Isenberg) names the resulting business thesis explicitly: **"the company brain is the moat."** [64:39–64:47 / 01:04:39–01:04:47] Cole develops this into a concrete, if speculative, business idea: instead of chasing publishing-cadence tactics (five posts a day, optimal posting time, buying retweets), a company should hire a genuinely thoughtful writer/journalist in or adjacent to its industry, pay them a substantial salary, and give them one job — think deeply and produce a high-quality long-form content library the rest of the organization (and AI tooling) can then repeat, remix, chop into short-form clips and distribute across every channel and department. [01:01:52–01:04:23] He draws a data-licensing analogy: companies are already paying large sums to acquire proprietary datasets (his example: a company buying Spirit Airlines' data after its bankruptcy); a personal or company content library is, functionally, the same kind of proprietary asset. [01:05:12–01:06:24] The host explicitly floats an unbuilt startup idea surfacing directly from this: **"company brains as a service."** [01:07:28]

### 7. The psychological/confidence angle: fear of publishing, "everyone has a voice"
Cole directly rebuts the idea that some people simply "lead uninteresting lives" and have nothing worth writing: **"that [is] incorrect... it['s] just wrong"** — everyone already operates as a "walking, talking personal language model" in ordinary conversation (sharing where you're from, what you're into, your life story at a coffee meeting); writing online is simply that same behavior made scalable and reusable via AI, rather than manually repeated meeting after meeting. [37:02–39:35] On people who say they dislike their own voice but admire someone else's, Cole's diagnosis is blunt: **"99% of the time," this isn't really a voice problem** — the person can't articulate what makes either voice distinct, and the underlying issue is fear of publishing at all, dressed up as a stylistic complaint. [56:41–57:03] He extends this to his most accomplished ghostwriting clients too: even people who have achieved major professional outcomes get "nervous staring at a blank page" and are afraid to put themselves out there — often over ideas ("know your customer") that are themselves totally uncontroversial commodity statements the vast majority of the world already agrees with. [59:37–01:00:27] His stated conclusion: most of this fear is self-inflicted, the fix is simply "getting out of your own way and hitting publish" so you can actually discover your own voice variables through practice, and — as a broader observation — most people are under-publishing relative to how much they're actually capable of producing. [01:00:27–01:01:00]

## Mechanisms, methods & implementation detail

- **"Approved language" recombination** (ghostwriting mechanic, generalized to AI): pull sentences/phrases the person has *already said* (book chapters, podcast transcripts, meeting recordings, prior posts) and recombine them into new pieces, rather than generating unapproved new claims. The larger the approved-language library, the more net-new combinations become possible without introducing anything the person hasn't actually said. [15:04–16:30]
- **Digital-brain repost/recombination use case**: feed AI two or more of your own already-published, already-approved pieces (e.g., two separate short posts on journaling benefits) and have it synthesize a new piece drawing only from that approved material — avoids AI "inventing" a voice or claims from nothing. [20:29–21:51]
- **Voice-diagnosis-and-fix technique**: when a draft "doesn't sound like" the person, isolate the *specific* variable that's off (word choice, or presence/absence of citations/data, or sentence structure/length) rather than rewriting wholesale; often a ~1% edit (e.g., adding 2–3 citation phrases) resolves the entire "this isn't me" complaint. [55:08–56:17]
- **Content-value self-check before writing**: before producing a piece, explicitly classify it against two independent axes — (a) commodity / personality / original, and (b) timely / timeless — and set distribution expectations accordingly rather than expecting every piece to perform the same way. [51:12–52:41]
- **Company-brain build**: hire one high-quality, thoughtful writer (a journalist-type hire) whose sole job is deep long-form thinking/writing about the company's industry; treat the resulting library as the input layer that AI/agents/social teams then repeat, remix and redistribute across every channel and short-form format. [01:01:52–01:04:23]
- **Point-of-view product design test** (SaaS): before building a new feature, ask whether it's accomplishing a generic task (commodity) or executing a specific, declared methodology (point of view) — Cole frames the second as the only version that compounds into a moat. [03:35–05:18]

## Key concepts & takeaways

- **The SaaS-moat reversal**: "SaaS has no moat in the AI era" is commonly asserted; Cole's reframe is that SaaS (and any product/content) has a moat only when it embodies a specific point of view that a copier cannot copy without also doing the underlying thinking.
- **The AI-slop reversal**: "AI slop" is commonly blamed on AI generation; Cole reframes it as commodity content — writing not attributable to any specific person's point of view — which can be produced by a human or an AI equally.
- **Commodity / personality / original** is the core content-value ladder: generic advice → personally-attributed perspective → genuinely new thinking crystallized only after prolonged focus on a narrow topic (Cole's example of "original" at the extreme: Pythagoras's six-word "the earth is round").
