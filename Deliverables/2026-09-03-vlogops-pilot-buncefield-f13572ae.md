## script

**1. scene** — derived from `beat-3`

At 06:01 on 11 December 2005, somebody pressed the fire alarm at Buncefield. That started the firewater pump. Then, almost immediately, came the explosion. The investigation said the pump was probably the ignition source. Not a thunderbolt. Not some cinematic rogue spark from the heavens. A pump, doing the thing pumps do, in a place now full of petrol vapour. Absolutely magnificent. The kind of engineering sequence that makes you put both hands on the table and stare at it.

By then, Tank 912 had already overflowed. Petrol had started coming out through roof vents at 05:37. More than 250,000 litres had escaped. In windless conditions, the vapour cloud spread about 360 metres. Petrol vapour does not need to be dramatic. It is dramatic already. It is fuel looking for a reason to become everybody's worst morning.

And this was not a little roadside tank with a bloke in a fluorescent jacket peering at a dipstick. Tank 912 held 6 million litres. Petrol had been delivered into it from 18:50 the night before. At 03:05, its automatic tank gauge flatlined. It stopped registering the rising level. Its three alarms could therefore never trigger. The people operating the site worked to alarms, so no alarm meant no warning.

Then came the final guard: an independent high-level switch, meant to give an alarm and automatically shut things down. It was inoperable. So the tank filled, the gauge said nothing, the final protection said nothing, the petrol went over the top, and a firewater pump arrived to light the whole appalling arrangement. You could not design a more expensive chain of tiny silences if you were trying.

**2. scene** — derived from `beat-2`

The last line of defence at Tank 912 was called the independent high-level switch. This was the grown-up backup. The thing you install because gauges fail, people are busy, nights are long and liquids remain stubbornly committed to gravity. When petrol got too high, it should have raised a final alarm and shut the operation down.

It did not work. And here is the extraordinary bit: it needed a padlock. Not a vault door. Not a bespoke control cabinet made by monks in a mountain laboratory. A padlock held the switch's check lever in the working position. Without it, after a periodic test, the lever could fall into the disabled position.

The switch had been fitted in July 2004. Its manufacturer, TAV, had not told the installer, Motherwell, or the site that the padlock mattered. Motherwell thought it was there merely to stop somebody tampering with the switch. So it was never fitted. Nobody on site knew it was important. The switch was tested regularly, which sounds reassuring right up until you discover that the test itself could leave the switch unable to do its job. That is not a test. That is a trapdoor with a clipboard.

There was even another tank, Tank 911, which had operated for at least nine months with no working independent high-level switch. After the incident, TAV's fix was a pin that stopped the lever falling. A pin. The whole immense machinery of petroleum storage, delivery systems, alarms, contractors, audits and formal safety arrangements had somehow got itself beaten by the absence of a small piece of metal whose principal talent was staying where it had been put.

The people in the control room did not create that arrangement. They inherited it. They had a safety device which appeared to exist, was periodically tested, and could quietly be left switched off by the very act of checking it. That is the sort of confidence a system wears right before reality takes a hammer to it.

**3. scene** — derived from `claim-5`

Buncefield did not lack paperwork. Paperwork was present. It had presumably enjoyed a perfectly respectable career. The problem was that the paperwork and the depot had become two different countries with no functioning border crossing.

A 2004 independent audit said contractor competence for safety-critical work should be evaluated. The later account said there appeared to have been little, if anything, done in response. The audits focused on whether a system was in place. They did not test the quality of the systems. Most importantly, they did not check whether the systems were actually being used or were effective. There it is: the administrative equivalent of checking that a smoke alarm has been purchased, then walking away before noticing it has no battery.

The safety report had a critical-parts list that was put together without any fundamental rationale. Management-of-change was not applied when the independent high-level switch was replaced. Parts of that safety report were described as aspirational rather than a true reflection of conditions on site. Aspirational. A lovely word. A word normally associated with brochures for flats that turn out to have a view of a skip. It is less charming when applied to equipment intended to stop petrol overflowing.

Meanwhile, throughput had risen four-fold. Supervisors worked 12-hour shifts, sometimes 84 hours in seven days. They kept a small alarm clock in the control room to remind them that tanks were approaching full. One requested a backup independent high-level switch. Shortly before the explosion, incoming flow rose from 550 to 900 cubic metres an hour without the supervisors' knowledge.

That little alarm clock is the whole story in miniature. An ordinary domestic object, drafted into helping humans watch an industrial system because the industrial system was not reliably watching itself. The night-shift staff were not the punchline. They were the people trying to operate a depot with the controls they had, under pressure they did not set, while the important bits above them were busy looking acceptable on paper.

**4. scene** — derived from `beat-4`

The explosion and fire at Buncefield became the largest peacetime fire in the UK. More than 20 tanks were engulfed. The fire burned for five days. More than 40 people were injured, and there were no fatalities. Fuel and foam leaked through failed bunds toward the drinking-water aquifer. For the people caught in and around it, that is the part where the clever commentary shuts up for a moment. A five-day fire is not an anecdote. It is a catastrophe that kept happening every hour it remained lit.

Then came the bill, because industrial failure is wonderfully democratic in one narrow respect: eventually it sends an invoice. At sentencing in July 2010, the judge said the failings had more to do with slackness, inefficiency and a more-or-less complacent approach to safety than cost-cutting.

Total UK was fined £2.6 million. HOSL was fined £1.45 million. BPA was fined £300,000. Motherwell was fined £1,000. TAV was fined £1,000. Together, those fines came to £5,352,000. Costs added another £4,081,000.

And set against that lot sits the detail that will not leave you alone: a high-level switch whose working position depended on a padlock that had never been fitted. The manufacturer had not explained its purpose. The installer thought it was anti-tamper. The site did not know it mattered. The device was tested, and the test could disable it.

Five days of fire. More than 20 tanks engulfed. £5,352,000 in fines. £4,081,000 in costs.
