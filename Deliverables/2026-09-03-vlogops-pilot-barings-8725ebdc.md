**1. scene** — derived from `claim-7`

By 27 February 1995, the hidden losses at Barings Futures Singapore had reached £827 million. Not £827,000. Not £8.27 million, which would already have made for an extremely uncomfortable Monday. £827 million. Then, including the cost of closing the positions out, the cumulative losses from the unauthorised trading totalled £927 million. That is the number at the end of this particular machine.

The remarkable thing is that the number had not appeared out of a hedge, fully formed and wearing a tiny bowler hat. It had grown. By the end of 1993, the hidden losses were already more than £20 million. By the end of 1994, they were more than £200 million. Then, by late February 1995, they had reached £827 million.

That is not a surprise. That is a building renovation conducted with dynamite. The figures had gone from alarming, to extraordinary, to the sort of number that makes a calculator look as though it needs a lie-down.

Barings collapsed at the end of February 1995. The official inquiry found that the losses had come from unauthorised and concealed trading at Barings Futures Singapore. The hidden position had not been noticed earlier because controls had failed seriously and management had become confused about who was supposed to be minding what.

And that is where this story began: not with a mysterious financial fog, not with an unknowable force of nature, but with a bank that had allowed a problem to grow in plain arithmetic.

**2. scene** — derived from `claim-2`

At the centre of the concealment was account No. 88888. It was not a glamorous bit of engineering. No brass plate, no enormous machine, no villainous control room with a red button under glass. It was an account number: 88888. Five eights. The sort of thing that sounds less like a banking record than a number selected by a fruit machine having a very good evening.

Nick Leeson opened it very rapidly after arriving at Barings Futures Singapore. The account was concealed from management in London, and it was used to hide losses. That was the hiding place: not a grand conspiracy vault beneath the City, but a record inside the operation where the records were supposed to tell the truth.

Banks run on records. That is the whole trick. Money moves, trades are made, balances change, and the institution needs a reliable account of what happened. The physical cash may be nowhere near the people typing the entries, but the entries are the dashboard. If the dashboard has been fitted with a second speedometer that only the driver can see, the journey becomes rather theoretical.

The Board of Banking Supervision found that the true position was not detected before Barings collapsed. External auditors, supervisors and regulators did not detect it either. Meanwhile, account No. 88888 sat inside the story doing the least cinematic and most consequential job imaginable: keeping losses out of sight.

A hidden account is not exciting. That is exactly why it is terrifying. Expensive disasters are often built from machinery so boring that nobody expects it to be the machinery that matters.

**3. scene** — derived from `claim-4`

There was one arrangement at Barings Futures Singapore that should have made everyone deeply uncomfortable: Leeson was not only a trader. He was also in charge of the back office. He effectively controlled both sides of the trading operation.

That meant the person making trades was also overseeing the settlement and recording of those trades. It was like letting the same person write the restaurant bill, approve the restaurant bill, and then assure the kitchen that nobody had ordered twelve lobsters. Not because any one person was automatically dishonest, but because the system had removed the ordinary argument between action and record.

The front office makes the trade. The back office settles and records it. They are meant to be separate because disagreement is useful. It is not friction for the sake of paperwork; it is the little mechanical scrape that tells an organisation whether the wheel is still attached.

In August 1994, Barings' internal audit review of Barings Futures Singapore recommended separating those duties. The internal audit had pointed to weaknesses in the controls and risk-management structure. Barings failed to implement the recommendation.

That is the small guardian in this story. Not a dazzling new system. Not a heroic intervention involving helicopters and a giant emergency lever. A separation of duties: one person trades, another person records and settles. An internal audit identified the weakness. The recommendation existed. Then it was not acted upon quickly enough, or not at all.

The people dealing with the operation were left inside a structure that had made the basic checking arrangement optional. And once the checking arrangement was optional, the concealed account had room to become a catastrophe.

**4. scene** — derived from `claim-5`

While the concealed position grew, money kept travelling from London to Singapore. London sent funds to Barings Futures Singapore with no independent check on whether the funding requests were valid. Management did not question, control or place limits on the high ongoing levels of funding required by the Singapore operation.

That is an extraordinary sentence because it is so calm. Funds were sent. Requests were not independently checked. The levels were high and ongoing. Every word is wearing sensible shoes, carrying a clipboard, and quietly walking past a fire.

Immediately before the collapse, funding to Barings Futures Singapore represented twice Barings Group capital. Twice. The parent bank had sent its Singapore unit funding equal to two times the group's capital. Elsewhere, management did not ask why it was lending more than £300 million to clients to trade while it had collateral of only about £31 million.

The reported Singapore profits were extraordinary. The official record said they should have been viewed as abnormal and questionable. They were not properly scrutinised.

None of this required anyone on the night shift to have a supernatural gift for detecting doom. It required the organisation to make its own records collide with one another: the funding requests, the collateral, the reported profits, the settlement records, the person doing two jobs that should have been separate. Instead, the parts passed one another like strangers on an escalator.

By 27 February 1995, the unreported losses had reached £827 million. Including closeout costs, the cumulative losses totalled £927 million.
